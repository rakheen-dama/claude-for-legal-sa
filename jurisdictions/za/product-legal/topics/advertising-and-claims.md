# Advertising and Claims — South African Framework

This overlay covers the South African regulatory framework for marketing claims, advertising, and promotional activities. It is loaded by marketing-claims-review, launch-review (category 7), and is-this-a-problem skills when jurisdiction = ZA.

---

## 1. Statutory framework

Three primary statutory provisions govern misleading marketing in South Africa:

### CPA s29 — misleading, deceptive, or fraudulent representations

A supplier must not market goods or services in a manner that is misleading, deceptive, or fraudulent, or that is reasonably likely to imply a false or misleading representation. This covers:

- Factual claims about product performance, features, or benefits.
- Price representations (including "was/now" pricing, discounts, "free" offers).
- Endorsements, testimonials, and implied third-party approval.
- Dark patterns that mislead consumers about the nature or consequences of their actions.
- In-product messaging, onboarding copy, and notification text — not limited to traditional advertising channels.

The evidentiary bar is low: the NCC need only show the representation was "reasonably likely to imply" a false or misleading meaning. Proof of actual consumer deception is not required.

### CPA s30 — bait marketing

A supplier may not advertise goods or services at a stated price if the supplier does not intend to offer them for supply at that price, or does not have reasonable grounds to believe they can be supplied at that price for a reasonable period and in reasonable quantities. Key elements:

- The supplier must have adequate supply to meet reasonably anticipated demand.
- "Limited stock" disclaimers do not automatically excuse bait marketing — the supplier must still have reasonable grounds to believe the stated price will be honoured.
- Time-limited offers must state the end date and honour the price during the stated period.

### CPA s41 — false, misleading, or deceptive representations

A supplier must not make a representation that is false or misleading in a material respect. This extends beyond marketing to any representation made to a consumer, including during sales conversations, onboarding, and in-product messaging. Specific prohibitions include:

- Falsely representing that goods or services have sponsorship, approval, or affiliation that they do not have.
- Falsely representing that goods are of a particular standard, quality, or grade.
- Misrepresenting the existence or nature of a guarantee.
- Misrepresenting the price advantage of goods or services.

### Enforcement

The NCC enforces CPA marketing provisions with penalties up to R1 million or 10% of annual turnover (s112), whichever is greater. The NCC can also issue compliance notices requiring immediate corrective action (s100), and non-compliance with a compliance notice is referred to the Consumer Tribunal.

Recent enforcement trends include increased scrutiny of:

- Digital subscription pricing (hidden fees, unclear auto-renewal terms).
- Discount claims without genuine price comparisons ("was R999" when never actually sold at that price).
- Influencer marketing without adequate disclosure of commercial relationships.
- "Free" product claims where the product requires a paid subscription or in-app purchases to function.

## 2. ARB Code of Advertising Practice

The Advertising Regulatory Board (ARB) administers a self-regulatory code, not a statute. However, the ARB Code is widely observed by South African businesses, and ARB rulings carry practical force: non-compliance results in ad withdrawal, media refusal to carry the advertisement, and reputational consequences.

The ARB Code is based on the International Code of Advertising and Marketing Communication Practice. Core principles require that advertising be:

- **Legal** — comply with all applicable law.
- **Decent** — not offensive to prevailing standards.
- **Honest** — not abuse the trust of consumers or exploit their credulity.
- **Truthful** — not mislead consumers by inaccuracy, ambiguity, exaggeration, or omission.

### 2.1 Substantiation requirements

All factual claims in advertising must be substantiated before publication. The ARB requires:

- **Documentary evidence** — substantiation must exist at the time the advertisement is published, not assembled after a complaint.
- **Independent and objective** — evidence from the advertiser's own internal testing is weaker than independent verification. Third-party studies, laboratory results, and peer-reviewed sources carry more weight.
- **Adequate** — the evidence must support the specific claim made. A study showing 20% improvement does not substantiate a claim of 50% improvement.
- **Current** — evidence must reflect current conditions. Outdated studies may not support current claims.

### Puffery exception

General claims of superiority that no reasonable consumer would take literally ("the best experience") are treated as puffery and do not require substantiation. However, the line between puffery and factual claims is narrow under SA standards:

- "The best project management tool" — likely puffery.
- "The fastest project management tool in South Africa" — factual, requires substantiation.
- "Used by more SA companies than any other tool" — factual, requires evidence.

### 2.2 Comparative advertising

Comparative advertising is permitted under the ARB Code but must be:

- **Fair** — the comparison must be made on a like-for-like basis.
- **Objective** — based on verifiable facts, not subjective opinion presented as fact.
- **Substantiated** — the same substantiation standard applies to comparative claims.
- **Not denigratory** — must promote the advertiser's own merits, not primarily attack the competitor.

For companies in a dominant market position, the Competition Act s8(1)(d) adds an additional layer: exclusionary conduct through comparative advertising that forecloses competitors may constitute abuse of dominance.

## 3. Claim taxonomy under SA law

| Claim type | Definition | SA substantiation standard | Example |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Puffery** | Subjective, exaggerated claims no reasonable consumer takes literally | None required | "An incredible experience for your team" |
| **Factual** | Objectively verifiable statement about product features, performance, or results | Documentary evidence before publication (ARB); CPA s29 compliance | "Reduces processing time by 40%" — requires independent testing data |
| **Comparative** | Claims of superiority or parity relative to a named or implied competitor | Like-for-like comparison, substantiated, not denigratory (ARB + Competition Act) | "Processes invoices 3x faster than [Competitor]" — requires head-to-head testing |
| **Implied** | Claims the consumer would reasonably infer from the overall impression | Same as factual — the implied meaning is tested, not the literal words | "Built for healthcare" implies regulatory compliance — must be able to substantiate implied meaning |
| **Absolute** | Unqualified superlative or certainty claims | Highest substantiation bar — must be literally true in all circumstances | "100% secure" — virtually impossible to substantiate; avoid or qualify |
| **Compliance/endorsement** | Claims of regulatory approval, certification, or compliance status | Must match actual status; specific compliance substantiated (ARB + CPA s29) | "POPIA compliant" — requires documented compliance programme, not merely aspiration |

## 4. Promotional competitions (CPA s36 + Lotteries Act)

### Lawful promotional competition vs unlawful lottery

This is the most legally significant distinction in SA promotional law. Getting it wrong creates criminal risk under the Lotteries Act 57 of 1997.

**Lawful promotional competition (CPA s36):** A competition conducted in the ordinary course of business to promote goods or services, provided:

1. **Free entry must be available** — there must be a genuine free alternative method of entry. No purchase necessary to enter.
2. **Written rules** — complete official rules must be available to all participants before or at the time of entry.
3. **Prize disclosure** — total prize value and allocation method must be disclosed.
4. **Winner determination** — by lot, skill, or a combination. Random draws are permitted if free entry is available.
5. **Independent oversight** — draws must be conducted or audited independently.
6. **No misleading representation** — the competition and its terms must comply with CPA s29.

**Unlawful lottery (Lotteries Act):** A scheme where entry requires consideration (payment, purchase, or other value), prizes are distributed by lot, and the outcome depends on chance. Operating an unlawful lottery is a criminal offence.

### The boundary

The critical question is whether a "purchase" is genuinely required to enter. If the free entry method is:

- Buried in terms and conditions that participants are unlikely to read, or
- Practically more difficult than the paid entry, or
- Not genuinely accessible (e.g., "send a hand-written letter to PO Box..."),

the competition may be re-characterised as an unlawful lottery despite the nominal free entry option.

### POPIA for entrant data

Personal information collected from competition entrants must comply with POPIA:

- Purpose limitation — data collected for the competition should not be repurposed for general marketing without separate consent (or reliance on POPIA s69 for existing customers).
- Retention — data should be deleted once the competition concludes and prize fulfilment is complete, unless separate consent is obtained.

## 5. Merchandise Marks Act

The Merchandise Marks Act 17 of 1941 prohibits:

- **False trade descriptions** — applying a false trade description to goods, including descriptions of quality, fitness for purpose, origin, or manufacturing process.
- **Origin claims** — false or misleading claims about the geographic origin or source of goods.
- **Protected marks** — unauthorised use of marks or emblems that are protected by Gazette notice (including SABS, government crests, national symbols).
- **Certification claims** — claiming SABS or NRCS certification without holding current valid certification.

### Relevance to product counsel

- Claims that a product is "proudly South African" or "made in South Africa" must be accurate regarding the origin of manufacture or substantial transformation.
- Claims of ISO, SABS, or other certification must match the current, valid certificate scope.
- Use of quality marks or trust marks without authorisation is an offence.
- The "Proudly South African" campaign mark is a registered certification mark requiring membership and compliance with the campaign's criteria for local content.

### Penalties

Contravention of the Merchandise Marks Act is a criminal offence. Penalties include fines and imprisonment. The Act is enforced by SAPS and the Department of Trade, Industry and Competition (dtic), with the South African Bureau of Standards (SABS) and the National Regulator for Compulsory Specifications (NRCS) providing technical input on certification claims.

## 6. Pricing and discount claims

### CPA requirements for pricing

- **All-inclusive pricing (CPA s23)** — the displayed price must include all taxes, fees, and charges. No additional charges may be added at checkout that were not disclosed upfront.
- **Itemised breakdown** — if a consumer requests it, the supplier must provide an itemised breakdown of the price.
- **Prepaid-to-postpaid conversion** — switching from a prepaid to postpaid model (or vice versa) must clearly disclose the total cost difference.

### Discount claim rules

| Claim type | Requirement | Common violation |
|---|---|---|
| "Was R999, now R299" | "Was" price must be a genuine price at which the product was offered for a reasonable period | Inflated reference price never actually charged |
| "Save 70%" | Percentage saving must be calculated from a genuine prevailing price | Calculated from an inflated or outdated price |
| "Limited time offer" | Must state the end date; offer must be honoured during the stated period | No end date; offer extended indefinitely |
| "Free" | Product must be genuinely free — no hidden cost, mandatory purchase, or paid upgrade required to use | "Free" product requires paid subscription for core features |
| "From R99/month" | The stated price must be available; most consumers must be able to obtain the "from" price | Only available on 36-month contract; most users pay more |

### ARB alignment

The ARB Code reinforces these statutory requirements. ARB rulings have specifically addressed inflated "was" pricing, misleading "free" claims, and percentage savings calculated from artificial reference prices. The ARB applies a "reasonable consumer" standard: would an ordinary consumer understand the claim in the way the advertiser intended?

## 7. High-risk flag checklist

| Flag | Why high-risk | What to check |
|---|---|---|
| **Misleading marketing (CPA s29-41 + ARB)** | Low evidentiary bar. ARB forces ad withdrawal. NCC penalties up to R1m or 10% turnover. | Overstated performance? "Free" with hidden charges? Dark patterns? Inflated discounts? Unsubstantiated comparative claims? |
| **Promotional competition non-compliance (CPA s36 + Lotteries Act)** | Criminal offence risk. Public-facing = fast escalation to NCC + social media. | Complete official rules? Entry genuinely free (not unlawful lottery)? POPIA-compliant data collection? Prizes honoured? |
| **"POPIA compliant" / "FSCA approved" claims** | Asserting regulatory compliance without substantiation is both misleading (CPA s29) and advertising violation (ARB). | Claim matches registration/approval status? Specific compliance substantiated? Current status or one-time certification? |
