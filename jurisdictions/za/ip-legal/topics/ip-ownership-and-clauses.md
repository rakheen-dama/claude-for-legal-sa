# IP Ownership and Clauses — South African Framework

This overlay covers SA employer ownership of IP, assignment formalities, moral rights, background vs foreground IP, publicly financed R&D, restraint of trade, and trade secret protection. Loaded by ip-clause-review and cold-start-interview when jurisdiction = ZA.

---

## 1. Employer ownership of copyright (Copyright Act s21(1)(d))

Under SA law, where an employee creates a work **in the course of their employment** under a **contract of service**, the employer is the first owner of copyright — unless the parties have agreed otherwise in the contract of employment.

### Key rules

| Scenario | Who owns copyright (default) |
|---|---|
| Employee creates work in ordinary course of employment | Employer |
| Employee creates work outside working hours and outside employment scope | Employee |
| Independent contractor creates a work for a client | **Contractor** (not the client — see s21(1)(c) for specific exceptions) |
| Computer program or article commissioned from a contractor | **Contractor**, unless a written assignment is executed |

**Section 21(1)(c) exception:** Where copyright subsists in a cinematograph film, a sound recording, a computer program, or a work made in the pursuance of a commission, the person who commissioned the work owns copyright — but only if the work falls within these specific categories and is commissioned as such. Outside these categories, the contractor owns.

**Practice rule:** Do not assume the client owns copyright in commissioned work. Check the category and whether a written assignment is in place. A professional services contract without an IP assignment clause leaves IP ownership with the contractor.

## 2. Comparison with US work-for-hire doctrine

| Feature | SA Copyright Act s21 | US 17 USC §101 (work made for hire) |
|---|---|---|
| Employment works | Employer owns where work is made in course of employment | Employer owns where work is prepared by an employee within the scope of employment |
| Commissioned works | Contractor owns (except specific s21(1)(c) categories) | Client owns ONLY if: (a) falls in 9 statutory categories AND (b) parties signed a written WMFH agreement |
| US §101 categories | Not applicable | Contribution to collective work, part of motion picture, translation, supplementary work, compilation, instructional text, test, answer material, atlas |
| Written requirement | Assignment must be in writing (s22(3)); no statutory list of qualifying categories | WMFH designation must be in a written agreement |

**Critical difference:** The US work-for-hire doctrine and the SA employer/contractor ownership rules operate differently. A work that qualifies as work made for hire under US law may not vest in the client under SA law without a written assignment. Global IP agreements should address SA specifically.

## 3. Assignment formalities (Copyright Act s22(3))

An assignment of copyright (or an exclusive licence) in SA is only valid if it meets the following requirements:

| Requirement | Detail |
|---|---|
| **In writing** | The assignment must be reduced to writing |
| **Signed by the assignor** | The assignor (the person giving up the rights) must sign |
| **No prescribed form** | No specific form required — a clear written statement of the rights being assigned and the parties is sufficient |

**Future works:** It is possible to assign copyright in **future works** in SA, but the clause must clearly identify the categories of future works to which it applies and must comply with s22(3) formalities.

**Defective assignments:** An oral agreement to assign copyright, or a term in an employment contract that simply says "all IP belongs to the employer" without meeting s22(3) formalities for any independent contractor scenario, may not effectively transfer copyright.

## 4. Moral rights (Copyright Act s20)

Moral rights remain with the author regardless of copyright ownership or assignment.

| Right | Description |
|---|---|
| **Paternity right** | The right to claim authorship — to have the work attributed to the author |
| **Integrity right** | The right to object to distortion, mutilation, or other modification of the work that is prejudicial to the author's honour or reputation |

**Cannot be assigned:** Moral rights cannot be assigned or transferred. They remain with the individual author.

**Can be waived:** An author can waive moral rights by express written consent. A waiver clause should be included in:
- Employment agreements where the employer will modify or adapt works
- Contractor and freelance agreements where the client needs flexibility to modify the work
- IP assignment agreements
- Creative services agreements

**Standard clause guidance:** Include a moral rights waiver consent clause. Do not assume moral rights are automatically waived by virtue of employment or payment.

## 5. Background and foreground IP

SA law does not impose a statutory distinction between background and foreground IP, but the distinction is important in practice for:
- Joint development agreements
- Research collaborations
- University/industry partnerships
- Software development agreements
- Manufacturing contracts

### Recommended contractual definitions

| Term | Recommended definition approach |
|---|---|
| **Background IP** | IP owned by a party prior to the commencement of the agreement or developed independently of the agreement's activities |
| **Foreground IP** | IP created in the course of, or directly resulting from, activities conducted under the agreement |
| **Sideground IP** | (Optional) IP developed independently during the agreement period but outside its scope |

**Ownership allocation:** Clearly specify who owns foreground IP (sole ownership or joint). Joint ownership in SA creates co-ownership — each co-owner can exploit the work independently but cannot assign or license without the other's consent (Copyright Act s22). This is often commercially inconvenient — consider a sole-ownership model with a licence-back, or a clear allocation based on contribution.

## 6. IP from Publicly Financed Research and Development (Act 51 of 2008)

The **Intellectual Property Rights from Publicly Financed Research and Development Act 51 of 2008** (IPR-PFRD Act) applies to IP arising from research or development **funded wholly or partly from public funds**.

### Key rules

| Rule | Detail |
|---|---|
| IP vests in the institution | IP arising from publicly financed R&D vests in the recipient of public funds (the institution), not in the funder or the researcher personally |
| Commercialisation obligation | Institutions must endeavour to commercialise the IP in a manner that benefits SA and its people |
| NIPP notification | The institution must disclose IP to the National Intellectual Property Management Office (NIPMO) |
| Ministerial assignment | In certain circumstances, the Minister may require assignment of IP to the state if the institution fails to commercialise |
| Inventor recognition | Researchers who create the IP are entitled to a share of commercialisation benefits |

**Relevance for contracts:** Any agreement with a SA university, science council (e.g., CSIR, SABS), or other publicly funded institution must account for the IPR-PFRD Act. Clients cannot assume that IP arising from a funded collaboration is freely assignable to them — the institution's IP policies and the Act will constrain ownership and commercialisation.

## 7. Restraint of trade

SA common law recognises **restraint of trade** agreements as valid and enforceable — but only to the extent they are reasonable.

### Enforceability test

A restraint of trade is enforceable if:

1. The employer has a **legitimate proprietary interest** to protect — trade secrets, confidential business information, established customer relationships, or key workforce stability
2. The restraint is **reasonable** in scope — the geographic area, duration, and restricted activities must not be broader than necessary to protect that interest
3. The restraint is **not contrary to public policy** — excessive restraints that prevent a person from earning a living will not be enforced

**Key cases:** Basson v Chilwan 1993 (3) SA 742 (A) sets the framework: courts balance the employer's legitimate interest against the employee's right to earn a living and the public interest in free competition.

**Garden leave:** SA courts may enforce garden leave clauses as an alternative to or in combination with post-employment restraints, but the same reasonableness analysis applies.

## 8. Trade secrets — no dedicated statute

**SA has no dedicated trade secrets statute** equivalent to the US Defend Trade Secrets Act (DTSA) or the EU Trade Secrets Directive.

### Sources of protection

| Source | What it covers |
|---|---|
| **Common-law confidentiality** | Information imparted in circumstances importing an obligation of confidence is protected against unauthorised disclosure or use |
| **Contract** | NDA / confidentiality agreements are the primary mechanism — well-drafted contractual obligations are the strongest protection layer |
| **Delict / unlawful competition** | Misappropriation of trade secrets may constitute wrongful competition or the delict of unlawful appropriation of confidential information |
| **Employment law** | Employees owe an implied duty of fidelity, which includes not disclosing confidential information. Explicit post-employment confidentiality obligations require a clause. |
| **Cybercrimes Act 19 of 2020** | Unauthorised access to computer systems to obtain trade secrets may constitute an offence (s2-6) — criminal enforcement layer |

### Operational controls — essential

Because there is no statutory registration or presumption of protection, **operational controls are critical:**

- Clearly mark confidential materials as "CONFIDENTIAL" or "PROPRIETARY"
- Limit access on a need-to-know basis
- Use NDAs with all employees, contractors, and external parties who access confidential information
- Implement technical access controls (passwords, encryption, access logs)
- Include clear confidentiality clauses in employment agreements, contractor agreements, and supply chain agreements
- Maintain a trade secret register (description, classification, holder, access list, date of creation)
- Exit procedures: revoke access immediately on termination, conduct exit interviews, obtain acknowledgement of ongoing obligations

## 9. IP contract clause checklist for SA agreements

| Clause | Requirement |
|---|---|
| IP ownership | Specify who owns foreground IP — do not rely on statutory default |
| Assignment of copyright | Written, signed by assignor (s22(3) formalities) |
| Moral rights waiver | Explicit written waiver by the author |
| Background IP licence | Grant a licence for the other party to use background IP as needed for the agreement's purpose |
| Confidentiality | Define confidential information broadly; specify obligations (no disclosure, no use beyond purpose, return/destruction) |
| Trade secrets | Classify, list categories if possible; specify remedies for breach |
| Restraint of trade (employment) | Geographic scope, duration, activities — ensure proportionate to legitimate interest |
| IP from publicly financed R&D | If applicable: reference IPR-PFRD Act obligations, institution's IP policy |
| Future works | Clearly identify categories of future works covered by the assignment |
| Sub-contractor chain | Ensure sub-contractors are bound by equivalent IP obligations |

## 10. What NOT to replicate from US practice

| US concept | Why it does not apply in SA |
|---|---|
| **Work made for hire (17 USC §101)** | A distinct US doctrine with a closed list of 9 qualifying categories for commissioned works. SA uses different rules — contractor owns copyright unless a written assignment is executed (s22(3)). Do not use US WMFH analysis for SA agreements. |
| **Defend Trade Secrets Act (DTSA) / 18 USC §1836** | SA has no federal trade secrets statute. Protection is via contract, common law, and delict. DTSA procedures (ex parte seizure, exemplary damages, attorney fees) are not available in SA. |
| **Inevitable disclosure doctrine** | US doctrine (adopted in some states) allowing employers to restrict former employees from new jobs where disclosure of trade secrets would be "inevitable." SA has not adopted this doctrine. Courts assess actual or threatened breach of confidence, not inevitability. |
| **Shop rights** | US patent doctrine giving employers a non-exclusive licence to use employee inventions made using company resources. SA law addresses this differently — generally employer owns any patent for an invention made in the scope of employment, subject to the employment contract. |
| **Joint work rules (17 USC §101)** | US joint work requires intent to merge contributions into a unitary whole. SA co-authorship rules differ. In SA, co-owners need each other's consent to assign or exclusively license — check the SA position before applying US joint work concepts. |
