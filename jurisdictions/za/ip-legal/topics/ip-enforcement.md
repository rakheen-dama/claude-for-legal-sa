# IP Enforcement — South African Framework

This overlay covers SA enforcement mechanisms: interdicts, damages, account of profits, Anton Piller orders, Counterfeit Goods Act criminal enforcement, forum, and costs. Loaded by cease-desist, infringement-triage, and fto-triage when jurisdiction = ZA.

---

## 1. Interdicts — the primary enforcement tool

In SA, the equivalent of a US injunction is an **interdict** (not an "injunction"). This is the primary civil remedy for IP infringement.

### Types of interdict

| Type | Description | Standard |
|---|---|---|
| **Final interdict** | Permanent prohibition on the infringer. Granted after a full hearing on the merits. | Clear right; ongoing infringement or reasonable apprehension of future infringement; no adequate alternative remedy. |
| **Interim interdict** | Temporary prohibition pending the outcome of main proceedings. Urgent in nature. | Prima facie right (lower standard — need not be clear, only arguable); well-grounded apprehension of irreparable harm; balance of convenience favouring the applicant; no adequate alternative remedy. |
| **Interdictum de libero homine exhibendo** | Not relevant to IP — applies to detention of persons. |  |

### Application process for interim interdict

1. File an **urgent application** in the High Court (motion proceedings under Uniform Rule 6(12))
2. Support with a **founding affidavit** — facts establishing the right, infringement, and urgency
3. The court may grant the interim interdict on an **ex parte basis** (without notice to the respondent) if notifying the respondent would defeat the purpose (e.g., risk of destruction of evidence)
4. A **return date** is set — the respondent has the opportunity to oppose the interdict on the return date
5. If opposed, the interdict becomes **contested** and the court hears both sides before deciding whether to make it final or discharge it

**Practitioner note:** Always use the term "interdict" in SA legal work. Using "injunction" is not incorrect but can create confusion with non-SA jurisdictions.

## 2. Damages — compensatory only

SA damages for IP infringement are **compensatory only**. There are no treble or punitive damages available in ordinary civil IP proceedings.

### Calculation methods

| Method | Description | When appropriate |
|---|---|---|
| **Lost profits** | The profits the plaintiff would have earned but for the infringement. Requires evidence of actual sales diverted. | Where the plaintiff and infringer compete directly in the same market |
| **Price erosion** | Reduction in the plaintiff's prices caused by the infringer's lower-priced competing goods. | Where market price was depressed by the infringement |
| **Reasonable royalty** | The licence fee that a willing licensor and willing licensee would have agreed in a hypothetical arm's-length negotiation at the date infringement began. | Where lost profits are difficult to establish; useful as a floor |
| **Account of profits** | Disgorgement of the infringer's profits attributable to the infringement. An alternative to damages — plaintiff must elect between damages and account of profits. | Where the infringer made substantial profits; useful when plaintiff's provable loss is low but infringer's gain is high |

**No double recovery:** The plaintiff elects between damages and account of profits — they cannot obtain both.

**Wasted costs:** In addition to the substantive remedy, the successful plaintiff recovers costs on the party-and-party scale (see costs section below).

## 3. Delivery up and destruction

The court may order:

- **Delivery up** of all infringing articles in the defendant's possession, custody, or control
- **Destruction** of infringing articles (where delivery up to the plaintiff is not appropriate)
- Delivery up of **implements** used to manufacture the infringing goods

Delivery up is particularly important in brand protection and counterfeit goods matters — it prevents the re-entry of infringing goods into commerce.

## 4. Anton Piller orders (search and seizure)

Where there is a real risk that the respondent will destroy evidence before proceedings can be served, a plaintiff may apply for an **Anton Piller order** — an ex parte order authorising the applicant and their attorneys to enter the respondent's premises and search for and seize specified evidence.

### Requirements for an Anton Piller order

All four requirements must be met:

| Requirement | What to show |
|---|---|
| **Strong prima facie case** | Clear and compelling evidence of infringement or misappropriation — not merely arguable |
| **Serious potential damage** | The infringement causes or will cause serious prejudice — mere technical infringement is insufficient |
| **Clear evidence that respondent has the material** | Specific evidence that the respondent possesses the documents, articles, or information sought |
| **Real risk of destruction** | Credible and specific evidence that the respondent is likely to destroy or conceal the material if notified of proceedings |

### Procedural requirements

- Application is made **ex parte** (without notice) — the element of surprise is essential
- A **supervising attorney** (independent from the plaintiff's attorneys) must accompany the search to protect the respondent's interests and ensure the order is not abused
- The supervising attorney records what is found and seized; the respondent must be given an opportunity to take legal advice before the search begins
- The order typically prohibits the respondent from interfering with the search and from informing third parties that the order has been granted

**Abuse risk:** Anton Piller orders are powerful and courts scrutinise applications carefully. Failure to disclose material facts (uberrimae fidei duty on ex parte applications) can result in the order being set aside and an adverse costs order against the applicant.

## 5. Counterfeit Goods Act 37 of 1997 — criminal enforcement

The **Counterfeit Goods Act** provides criminal enforcement mechanisms for trademark and copyright counterfeiting. It complements civil proceedings and enables supply-chain disruption.

### Criminal enforcement steps

| Step | Who is involved | What happens |
|---|---|---|
| **Lay a complaint** | Rights holder with SAPS or a CIPC inspector | File a formal complaint with supporting evidence of counterfeiting |
| **Obtain search warrant** | SAPS or inspector (on application to magistrate) | Magistrate issues warrant authorising entry, search, and seizure |
| **Conduct raid** | Inspector and/or SAPS, with rights holder representative present | Search the premises, seize suspected counterfeit goods, arrest suspects if applicable |
| **Chain of custody** | Inspector / SAPS | Seized goods are catalogued, stored securely, and samples retained as evidence |
| **Criminal prosecution** | NPA (National Prosecuting Authority) | Prosecution for criminal offences under the Act |

### Criminal offences (Counterfeit Goods Act s2-3)

It is a criminal offence to:
- Manufacture, produce, package, sell, offer for sale, distribute, export, or import counterfeit goods
- Possess counterfeit goods for trade purposes
- The offence applies where the person **knows or reasonably suspects** the goods are counterfeit

### Rights holder's role

Rights holders can:
- Lay complaints and provide evidence
- Attend raids to assist in identifying infringing goods (need someone with authority to authenticate the genuine goods)
- Provide affidavits to support prosecution
- Pursue concurrent civil proceedings for interdicts and damages

### Combined civil and criminal strategy

| Layer | Purpose | Timing |
|---|---|---|
| Criminal enforcement (Counterfeit Goods Act) | Supply-chain disruption — remove goods from market, deter manufacturers | Can proceed simultaneously with civil proceedings |
| Civil interdict (Trade Marks Act / Copyright Act) | Long-term prohibition on the infringer | File in High Court; interim interdict before or concurrently with raid |
| Civil damages | Compensation for losses | After infringement established |
| Delivery up | Remove remaining infringing stock | Civil remedy |

## 6. Forum

| Type of matter | Forum | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Trademark infringement | High Court (any division with jurisdiction) | TMA s34 — civil proceedings in the High Court |
| Copyright infringement | High Court | Copyright Act s24 |
| Design infringement | High Court | Designs Act |
| Patent infringement | **Commissioner of Patents** | A High Court judge sitting in the capacity of Commissioner of Patents. Patent Act s65. Not a separate tribunal — sits in the High Court. |
| Patent revocation | Commissioner of Patents | Patents Act — revocation on grounds of invalidity |
| Compulsory licence application | Commissioner of Patents | Patents Act s56 |
| Anton Piller application | High Court | Urgent ex parte application |
| Counterfeit goods criminal enforcement | Magistrates' Court (criminal) | After arrest and charge |
| CIPC opposition (TM) | CIPC — Trade Marks Directorate | Before registration; adversarial before registrar, with possible High Court review |

## 7. Costs — loser pays

SA follows the **loser-pays** costs principle. The unsuccessful party in civil IP litigation pays the successful party's costs, taxed on the **party-and-party scale** (reasonable and necessary costs).

| Scale | When | Typical effect |
|---|---|---|
| **Party-and-party** | Standard — unsuccessful party pays | Partial recovery of the winner's actual legal costs |
| **Attorney-and-client** | Punitive — abuse of process, misconduct, contractual entitlement | Higher recovery; often ordered where the other party brought hopeless proceedings |
| **Each party bears own costs** | Court's discretion; mixed results; early settlement | No costs recovery |

**Strategic implication:** The loser-pays rule increases the stakes of IP litigation in SA. A defendant who successfully defends an infringement action will recover costs; a plaintiff who loses a poorly founded action may face a double cost burden (own costs + opponent's costs). Advise clients on costs exposure at the outset of each matter.

**Assessment:** Costs are assessed by the **taxing master** after the litigation, applying prescribed tariffs. Not all costs incurred are recoverable — only those reasonably necessary for the litigation.

## 8. What NOT to replicate from US practice

| US concept | Why it does not apply in SA |
|---|---|
| **Treble/enhanced damages** | SA damages are compensatory only. Courts may not award treble or enhanced damages in civil IP proceedings. 35 USC §284 (patent) and 15 USC §1117 (trademark) enhancements are US statutory — no SA equivalent. |
| **Punitive damages** | Not available in SA private-law IP proceedings. SA delict is compensatory. |
| **ITC Section 337 exclusion orders** | US International Trade Commission exclusion orders preventing import of infringing goods. SA has no equivalent. Border enforcement relies on SARS customs and the Counterfeit Goods Act. |
| **TRO under DTSA (18 USC §1836)** | US ex parte seizure order specific to trade secrets under the federal statute. SA uses Anton Piller orders from the High Court — common law, broader scope. |
| **19 USC customs seizure** | US Customs and Border Protection seizure of counterfeit goods based on recorded trademark/copyright registrations. SA uses SARS customs combined with Counterfeit Goods Act enforcement. |
| **Willfulness requirement for enhanced damages** | US patent/copyright law requires finding of willful infringement to unlock enhanced remedies. SA has no equivalent gateway — damages are always compensatory regardless of willfulness. |
| **Permanent injunction (eBay v MercExchange standard)** | US four-factor test for permanent injunctions in patent cases. SA uses the SA interdict standard — different requirements, no eBay analysis. |
| **Jury trial in IP matters** | SA has no jury system. IP cases are heard by a judge sitting alone (or in some cases by the Commissioner of Patents). |
