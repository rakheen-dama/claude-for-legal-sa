# Fundamental Transactions — South African Framework

This overlay covers Companies Act Chapter 5 Part A fundamental transactions — disposal of assets (s112), amalgamation or merger (s113), and scheme of arrangement (s114) — plus Competition Commission merger control and related regulatory requirements. It is loaded by closing-checklist, diligence-issue-extraction, integration-management, and material-contract-schedule when jurisdiction = ZA.

---

## 1. Statutory framework

Companies Act 71 of 2008, Chapter 5, Part A establishes three categories of fundamental transactions:

1. **Disposal of all or the greater part of assets or undertaking** (s112)
2. **Amalgamation or merger** (s113)
3. **Scheme of arrangement** (s114)

All three share common procedural requirements:

- **Special resolution** per s115 — requires ≥75% of voting rights exercised at a meeting where ≥25% of all voting rights are represented.
- **Solvency and liquidity test** (s4) — applies to mergers (s113) and must be satisfied by each merging company immediately after the transaction.
- **Appraisal rights** (s164) — available to dissenting shareholders in all three transaction types.
- **TRP compliance** — if the transaction involves a regulated company (s118), the Takeover Regulation Panel must issue a compliance certificate before implementation.

The fundamental transaction provisions replaced the old Companies Act 61 of 1973 sections on schemes of arrangement (which required court approval) and disposals. The 2008 Act deliberately removed the requirement for court sanction of schemes of arrangement, making all three transaction types board- and shareholder-driven processes subject to regulatory oversight rather than court oversight.

## 2. Disposal of all or greater part of assets (s112)

### Threshold

A disposal constitutes a fundamental transaction when it involves:

- More than **50% of the gross assets** of the company, fairly valued irrespective of liabilities, **or**
- More than **50% of the undertaking** of the company, fairly valued.

The "fairly valued" requirement means that the assets must be valued at fair market value, not book value. This is a factual determination that may require independent valuation.

### Key characteristics

1. **Not an automatic vesting** — unlike a merger under s113, a disposal under s112 does not cause automatic transfer of assets by operation of law. Each asset must be separately transferred according to the applicable transfer mechanism (e.g., registration of immovable property at the Deeds Office, cession of claims, delivery of movables).
2. **Separate transfer of each asset** — share transfers require STRATE processing (for listed securities) or share register amendment (for unlisted). IP requires CIPC recordal. Immovable property requires Deeds Office registration.
3. **Liabilities do not transfer automatically** — unless the purchaser assumes them by agreement, the disposing company retains all liabilities.

### Holding company cascade

If a subsidiary disposes of assets and that disposal constitutes a disposal of all or the greater part of the **holding company's** assets or undertaking, the holding company's shareholders must also approve the transaction by special resolution (s115(2)(b)). This cascade ensures that shareholders at the ultimate holding company level cannot be bypassed through subsidiary-level transactions.

### Process

1. Board resolves to propose the disposal to shareholders.
2. Notice to shareholders per s115(2): at least 15 business days before the meeting, including a summary of the transaction terms.
3. Shareholder meeting: special resolution (≥75% of votes exercised).
4. If TRP-regulated: obtain compliance certificate.
5. Execute individual asset transfer agreements.
6. Complete transfer formalities for each asset category.

## 3. Amalgamation or merger (s113)

### Definition

A merger or amalgamation is a transaction in which the assets and liabilities of two or more companies are combined. The Companies Act recognises two forms:

- **(a) New entity merger** — all merging companies are dissolved and their assets and liabilities vest in a newly incorporated company.
- **(b) Absorption merger** — one or more companies are absorbed into a surviving company, with the absorbed companies being dissolved.

### Merger agreement

The merging companies must enter into a merger agreement that sets out:

- The terms and means of effecting the merger.
- The proposed MOI of the surviving or new company.
- The manner and basis of converting or exchanging securities.
- Any payment to holders of securities.
- Details of any proposed allocation of assets or liabilities that are not to vest in the surviving or new company.

### Automatic vesting

This is the critical distinction from a s112 disposal: upon registration of the merger by the CIPC, **all assets and liabilities of each merging company vest in the surviving or new company by operation of law**. No separate transfer of individual assets is required. This includes:

- Immovable property (though Deeds Office registration must follow to update the register)
- Contractual rights and obligations (no counterparty consent required)
- IP registrations (though CIPC recordal is needed)
- Licences and permits (subject to the terms of the licence)

### Solvency and liquidity test

Each merging company must satisfy the solvency and liquidity test (s4) as at the effective date of the merger:

1. **Solvency** — the assets of the company, fairly valued, equal or exceed its liabilities, fairly valued.
2. **Liquidity** — the company will be able to pay its debts as they become due in the ordinary course of business for a period of 12 months after the date on which the test is applied.

The board of each merging company must issue a certificate confirming that the test is satisfied.

### Creditor protection

No creditor consent is required for a merger, but s116 provides a **15 business day objection window**. The company must publish a notice of the proposed merger, and any creditor may apply to court for a review of the transaction on the grounds that the creditor's interests will be materially and adversely affected. The court may order adequate security for the creditor's claim.

### CIPC filing

The surviving or new company must file a notice of the merger with the CIPC for:

- **Registration** of the surviving or new company (or amendment of its registration details).
- **Deregistration** of any company that ceases to exist.

## 4. Scheme of arrangement (s114)

### Nature

A scheme of arrangement is an arrangement proposed by a company between itself and the holders of any class of its securities. It is the most flexible of the three fundamental transaction types and is commonly used for:

- **Public company acquisitions** — the most common structure for taking a listed company private or acquiring control.
- **Delistings** — scheme to acquire all listed shares and delist from the JSE.
- **Unbundlings** — distribution of shares in a subsidiary to shareholders.
- **Reorganisations** — internal restructurings, share consolidations, or reclassifications.

### Key features

1. **No court approval required** — unlike the pre-2008 Act position, a scheme under s114 does not require court sanction. This was a deliberate policy choice to reduce costs and delays.
2. **Special resolution** per s115 — the same voting thresholds and procedures apply as for the other fundamental transactions.
3. **TRP compliance** — if the scheme involves a regulated company (s118), a compliance certificate is required. This is typically the case for public company acquisitions.
4. **Board proposal** — the board proposes the scheme to shareholders, accompanied by all information reasonably required for shareholders to decide.

### Process for a public company acquisition by scheme

1. Offeror and target agree terms → scheme circular prepared.
2. TRP review of the circular and independent expert report.
3. Shareholder meeting: special resolution by target shareholders (≥75% of votes exercised).
4. If ≥15% oppose: potential court review under s115(3).
5. TRP issues compliance certificate.
6. Consideration paid to target shareholders.
7. Target shares transferred to offeror (or cancelled and reissued).
8. If delisting: JSE termination of listing.

## 5. Shareholder approval (s115)

### Voting requirements

| Requirement | Threshold |
|---|---|
| Resolution type | Special resolution |
| Approval threshold | ≥75% of voting rights **exercised** |
| Quorum | ≥25% of **all** voting rights entitled to be exercised |
| Acquiring party exclusion | Votes of the acquiring party are excluded from both quorum and voting calculations (s115(4)) |

### Court review triggers

Section 115(3) provides two avenues for dissenting shareholders to seek court review:

1. **Automatic trigger** — if holders of **≥15% of the voting rights exercised** at the meeting voted against the resolution, **and** within **5 business days** any dissenting shareholder requires the company to seek court approval, the company may not proceed without court approval (s115(3)(a)).

2. **Individual application** — within **10 business days** after the vote, any dissenting shareholder may apply to the court for leave to apply for a review, regardless of the percentage of opposing votes (s115(3)(b)).

### Grounds for court to set aside

The court may set aside the resolution **only** if:

- The resolution is **manifestly unfair** to any class of holders of the company's securities, **or**
- The vote was **materially tainted** by:
  - Conflict of interest
  - Inadequate disclosure of material information
  - Procedural irregularity

### Company option

If the court review is triggered, the company may choose to treat the resolution as a nullity rather than seeking court approval (s115(5)). This allows the company to abandon the transaction without the cost and delay of court proceedings.

## 6. Appraisal rights (s164)

### Availability

Appraisal rights are available to shareholders who dissent from:

- A disposal under s112
- A merger under s113
- A scheme of arrangement under s114
- An amendment to the MOI that materially and adversely affects the rights or interests of shareholders

### Requirements for claiming appraisal rights

1. **Advance notice** — the shareholder must have given the company written notice of their intention to oppose the resolution **before** the meeting at which the vote is taken.
2. **Attendance and vote** — the shareholder must have been present at the meeting (in person or by proxy) and **voted against** the resolution.
3. **Demand** — the shareholder must send a written demand for payment of fair value within **20 business days** after the resolution is adopted.

### Fair value determination process

| Step | Action | Timeline |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Shareholder sends demand | Within 20 BD after resolution |
| 2 | Company makes written offer of fair value | Within 5 BD of receiving demand |
| 3 | Shareholder accepts or rejects offer | Within 30 BD of receiving offer |
| 4 | If rejected: either party may apply to court | No statutory deadline |
| 5 | Court determines fair value | As per court process |

### Key principles

- "Fair value" is not defined in the Act — courts have adopted a willing buyer-willing seller approach, considering all relevant factors including synergy value in appropriate cases.
- Payment of fair value **does not constitute a distribution** for purposes of the solvency and liquidity test (s164(17)). This means the company can pay dissenting shareholders without satisfying the distribution requirements of s46.
- The company bears the costs of the court application unless the court orders otherwise.

## 7. Competition Commission merger control

### Three-tier notification system

South African merger control operates under the Competition Act 89 of 1998 with three tiers based on combined annual turnover and asset values. The thresholds and filing fees below took effect on **1 May 2026** (GN 7029 GG 54020). For the authoritative current figures and the pre-1-May-2026 values (for transactions notified earlier), see `statutes/competition.yaml` → `intermediate_merger_threshold_2026`, `large_merger_threshold_2026`, and the corresponding `*_filing_fee_2026` keys.

| Tier | Combined turnover/assets | Target turnover/assets | Filing fee | Timeline |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Small merger** | Below R1bn combined **or** below R200m target | Below R200m | None (no statutory fee) | No prescribed period |
| **Intermediate merger** | ≥R1bn combined **and** ≥R200m target | ≥R200m | R220,000 | 20 BD (extendable by 40 BD) |
| **Large merger** | ≥R9.5bn combined **and** ≥R280m target | ≥R280m | R735,000 | 40 BD (extendable by 15 BD per extension) |

The pre-1-May-2026 thresholds and fees are preserved as closed entries in `statutes/competition.yaml` (`intermediate_merger_threshold`, `large_merger_threshold`, and the `*_filing_fee` keys) for transactions notified before that date.

### Key principles

1. **Suspensory** — intermediate and large mergers may not be implemented before approval. Implementation before approval ("gun-jumping") is a criminal offence carrying fines up to 10% of the merged entity's annual turnover.
2. **Mandatory notification** — intermediate and large mergers must be notified. Failure to notify is a separate offence.
3. **Small merger call-in** — the Commission may require notification of a small merger within 6 months of implementation if the merger may substantially prevent or lessen competition.

### Public interest assessment

Section 12A(3) of the Competition Act requires that public interest factors be considered on an **equal footing** with competition assessment:

- **Employment** — effect on employment levels in the relevant industry or region.
- **SMMEs** — ability of small and medium enterprises to become competitive.
- **Sector/region** — ability of a particular industrial sector or region to compete.
- **International competitiveness** — ability of national industries to compete internationally.
- **Greater spread of ownership** — promotion of a greater spread of ownership, in particular to increase the levels of ownership by historically disadvantaged persons (HDPs).

### National security review

Section 18A of the Competition Act provides for national security review. The Minister of Trade, Industry and Competition may, by notice in the Government Gazette, prohibit or impose conditions on a merger that involves a foreign acquiring firm and that raises national security concerns. This power is discretionary and not subject to the same timelines as the competition assessment.

## 8. B-BBEE conditions

### Ownership dilution risk

When a BEE shareholder exits as part of a fundamental transaction, the following risks arise:

1. **B-BBEE scorecard impact** — loss of ownership element points, which flow through to overall B-BBEE level.
2. **Government contract risk** — loss of preferred supplier status if B-BBEE level drops below the minimum required for the relevant contract.
3. **Sector-specific non-compliance** — breach of sector charter minimum ownership thresholds (e.g., Mining Charter: 26% for existing rights holders, 30% for new mining right applicants).

### Competition Commission "greater spread of ownership"

The Competition Commission assesses the effect of every merger on the greater spread of ownership (Competition Act s12A(3)(e)). If the transaction has a **neutral or negative effect** on HDP ownership, the Commission may:

- Impose conditions requiring the merged entity to establish an ESOP or HDP ownership scheme.
- Require disposal of a portion of shares to an HDP entity.
- Prohibit the merger if public interest concerns (including ownership spread) outweigh the competition benefits.

### Replacement BEE shareholder

A replacement BEE shareholder is often included as a **condition precedent** to the transaction. Due diligence must verify:

- The proposed replacement BEE shareholder qualifies as an HDP entity.
- The ownership arrangement does not constitute **fronting** under B-BBEE Act s13F.
- The economic interest and voting rights of the BEE shareholder are genuine and meaningful.
- There are no side agreements that dilute the BEE shareholder's interest or shift economic risk disproportionately.

### Fronting indicators

The B-BBEE Act 46 of 2013, s13F, prohibits fronting practices. Indicators include:

- Nominee or token arrangements where the BEE shareholder has no meaningful participation.
- Side agreements that guarantee returns to the BEE shareholder while eliminating downside risk.
- Excessive management fees or service agreements that extract value from the BEE shareholding.
- Lack of board representation or governance participation by BEE shareholders.
- Fronting is a criminal offence carrying fines up to 10% of annual turnover and/or imprisonment up to 10 years.

## 9. LRA s197 — automatic employee transfer

### Principle

When a business is transferred as a going concern, section 197 of the Labour Relations Act 66 of 1995 provides for the **automatic transfer of employees** from the old employer (transferor) to the new employer (transferee).

### Key rules

1. **Automatic transfer** — employees employed by the old employer at the time of transfer automatically become employees of the new employer. No employee consent is required.
2. **Terms and conditions preserved** — the new employer is bound by all terms and conditions of employment that applied immediately before the transfer, including:
   - Contracts of employment
   - Remuneration and benefits
   - Leave entitlements
   - Length of service (continuous service is preserved)
3. **Joint and several liability** — the old and new employers are **jointly and severally liable** for **12 months** after the transfer for any obligation arising from the employment relationship before the transfer.
4. **Collective agreements** — the new employer is bound by existing collective agreements and arbitration awards for their remaining term.
5. **No dismissal by reason of transfer** — an employee may not be dismissed merely because of the transfer. Such a dismissal would be automatically unfair under s187(1)(g).

### Due diligence implication

Because all employment terms transfer automatically by operation of law, due diligence for any fundamental transaction involving a transfer of business must capture:

- All employment contracts and letters of appointment.
- Salary scales, bonus schemes, and incentive arrangements.
- Pension and provident fund memberships and rules.
- Medical aid contributions and post-retirement medical benefits.
- Leave balances and long-service awards.
- Bargaining council agreements and recognition agreements.
- Any pending disciplinary proceedings, grievances, or CCMA referrals.
- Employment equity plans and reports.

Failure to identify these obligations before closing means the new employer inherits them regardless — there is no "buyer beware" defence against automatic transfer.
