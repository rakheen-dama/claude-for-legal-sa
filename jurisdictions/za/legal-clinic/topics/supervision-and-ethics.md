# Supervision and Ethics — South African Law Clinic Overlay

This overlay covers supervision obligations, professional ethics, legal professional privilege, and data protection obligations specific to South African law clinics. It is loaded by supervision-check, privilege-assessment, document-review, and AI-output-review skills when jurisdiction = ZA.

---

## 1. SA Supervision Model — Framework and Key Distinctions

### 1.1 Statutory Basis

Supervision in South African law clinics is governed by a hierarchy of instruments:

1. **LPA s34(8)(b)(i)** — services must be "rendered by or under the supervision of attorneys."
2. **LPC Rule 37.1.1** — direct personal supervision by an admitted attorney is mandatory.
3. **LPC Code of Conduct clauses 3.11, 3.15** — the supervising attorney remains personally responsible for work quality and professional standards.
4. **PVTC terms** — the contract itself specifies the supervision obligations the clinic undertakes toward the candidate.

There is no SA equivalent of a "student practice rule" framework or a bar ethics opinion regime that can modify or relax these statutory obligations.

### 1.2 Comparison with US/ABA Model

| Aspect | South Africa | United States (ABA model) |
|---|---|---|
| Supervision standard | "Direct personal supervision" — LPC Rule 37.1.1 | Varies by state; ABA Model Rule 5.3 (non-lawyer supervision); student practice rules per jurisdiction |
| Who supervises | Admitted attorney on professional staff of the clinic | Supervising attorney or clinical faculty (may be non-admitted faculty at some institutions) |
| Student/candidate status | Candidate legal practitioner under PVTC — undergoing vocational training; not yet admitted | "Law student" — not admitted; practice permitted only under student practice rules |
| Relaxation of supervision | Not permitted — LPA s34(8) is prescriptive; no regulatory carve-outs | Some jurisdictions permit limited unsupervised practice under student rules |
| AI in legal practice guidance | No SA equivalent of ABA Formal Op. 512 (2024) | ABA Formal Op. 512 (2024) addresses attorney obligations when using AI |
| Ethics opinion body | No SA equivalent of state bar ethics opinion infrastructure | State bars issue ethics opinions; ABA issues formal and informal opinions |

Key implication: US clinic materials and training resources must not be applied in a SA clinic without checking whether the underlying rule or ethics opinion has a SA equivalent. Many US student practice concepts have no SA counterpart.

### 1.3 "Direct Personal Supervision" — Operational Meaning

LPC Rule 37.1.1 requires "direct personal supervision" by an admitted attorney. This standard means:

1. **Availability** — the supervisor must be accessible during working hours to review candidate work, answer questions, and make real-time professional judgements. An attorney who is in another city and reviews work only by email does not satisfy this requirement.
2. **Awareness** — the supervisor must be aware of every matter the candidate is handling, including the current status, any upcoming deadlines, and any risk indicators.
3. **Responsibility** — the supervisor is personally and professionally accountable for the work product delivered to clients, regardless of whether the candidate drafted it. The supervisor cannot disclaim responsibility by saying the candidate "handled the matter independently."
4. **Substitution** — if the designated supervisor is absent for more than a few days, a replacement admitted attorney must be available and must take active responsibility for the candidates under supervision.

## 2. LPA s34(8)(b)(i) — "Under Supervision" — Detailed Analysis

### 2.1 What "Under Supervision" Requires

The phrase "rendered by or under the supervision of attorneys" in LPA s34(8)(b)(i) has been interpreted consistently in LPC guidance to impose three cumulative obligations on the supervising attorney:

1. **Available** — physically or immediately accessible to review work and give directions.
2. **Aware** — briefed on the substance of each matter being handled by the candidate, not merely nominally listed as supervisor on the file.
3. **Responsible** — has reviewed and approved client-facing output (letters, court documents, advice memoranda, settlement agreements) before it is delivered to the client or filed.

### 2.2 Supervision of Client-Facing Output

Every document that goes to a client, opposing party, or court must pass through a supervision gate before dispatch:

| Output type | Supervision requirement |
|---|---|
| Client advice letters | Reviewed and approved by supervising attorney; signed by supervisor or countersigned below candidate's signature |
| Letters to opposing parties | Signed by admitted attorney or clearly countersigned; candidate's status may be disclosed |
| Court pleadings and notices | Signed by admitted attorney; candidate may prepare but not independently file |
| Settlement agreements | Reviewed and signed off by supervising attorney |
| Oral advice to clients | Candidate may provide under direct supervision; complex matters require attorney present |

### 2.3 Disclosure of Candidate Status in Correspondence

All correspondence must make clear that the author is a candidate legal practitioner and that the work is being done under the supervision of an admitted attorney. This is not merely an ethical courtesy — it is required by the framework:

- Letters signed by candidates should include a line such as: "This letter is written under the supervision of [Name], admitted attorney, on behalf of the [Clinic Name]."
- A candidate may not hold themselves out as an admitted attorney. Using "attorney" in a signature block without qualification is misleading and may constitute a representation of admission that has not occurred.
- Correspondence sent on clinic letterhead that does not identify the candidate's status creates potential for the client to rely on the work as if it were the work of an admitted attorney without supervision.

Compare US requirements: US student practice rules typically require explicit disclosure of the student's law-student status. SA requires identification of the supervising admitted attorney, not merely the candidate's training status.

## 3. Legal Professional Privilege in South Africa

### 3.1 Two Forms of Privilege

South African law recognises two distinct forms of legal professional privilege, both recognised at common law and confirmed in post-Constitution jurisprudence:

| Form | Scope | Key case |
|---|---|---|
| Legal advice privilege | Communications between a legal adviser acting in a professional capacity and a client, made in confidence, for the dominant purpose of seeking or giving legal advice. Privilege belongs to the client. | *Tulipwax SA (Pty) Ltd v Holden* (established the professional capacity requirement) |
| Litigation privilege | Communications created for the dominant purpose of pending or reasonably contemplated litigation. Broader scope — covers communications with third parties (e.g., expert witnesses, investigators). | *Ibex RSA Holdco (Pty) Ltd v Tiso Blackstar Group (Pty) Ltd* (adopted dominant purpose test) |

### 3.2 Legal Advice Privilege — Requirements

For legal advice privilege to apply, all three elements must be satisfied:

1. **Qualified legal adviser** — the communication must be with a legal adviser acting in their professional (not personal or commercial) capacity. The adviser must be an admitted attorney or advocate in SA. Candidate legal practitioners are not yet admitted — communications directed solely to a candidate, without supervisor involvement, may not attract privilege.
2. **Confidentiality** — the communication must have been made in confidence and must have been kept confidential. Disclosure (even inadvertent) may waive privilege.
3. **Dominant purpose** — the dominant purpose of the communication must be seeking or giving legal advice, not commercial or administrative matters.

### 3.3 Litigation Privilege — Requirements

Litigation privilege applies to:

1. Documents or communications created for the **dominant purpose** of pending or reasonably contemplated litigation (applying the dominant purpose test from *Ibex RSA Holdco*).
2. Communications with **third parties** (e.g., medical experts, forensic accountants, investigators) where the dominant purpose is to obtain information or evidence for use in the litigation.
3. The litigation must be **reasonably contemplated** — not merely a speculative possibility.

### 3.4 What SA Privilege Does Not Cover

South African law does **not** recognise:

- **Work-product doctrine** — there is no SA equivalent of the US Federal Rule of Civil Procedure 26(b)(3) work-product doctrine. Documents prepared by an attorney in anticipation of litigation are covered (if at all) only by litigation privilege, which has stricter requirements.
- **Attorney-client privilege as an absolute rule** — SA privilege is subject to constitutional override in exceptional cases (National Director of Public Prosecutions applications, Section 205 Criminal Procedure Act subpoenas).

### 3.5 In-House Counsel Privilege

Legal professional privilege extends to in-house legal advisers (*Mohamed NO v President of the Republic of South Africa*), but only when the in-house counsel is acting in a legal (as opposed to commercial or administrative) capacity. An in-house attorney who advises the organisation on business strategy rather than legal rights and obligations is not attracting privilege for that advice.

### 3.6 Privilege as a Shield, Not a Sword

SA privilege is a "negative right" — it shields a party from being compelled to disclose communications. It is not a "positive right" that can be used to suppress documents once confidentiality has been broken. If privileged communications have been disclosed (even improperly), the privilege may have been waived and the documents may be used by the receiving party.

## 4. AI Output and Privilege in South African Law Clinics

### 4.1 AI Output is Not Automatically Privileged

AI-generated output does not attract legal professional privilege in South Africa. The reasons:

1. **Legal advice privilege cannot attach** — AI is not a qualified legal adviser. It is not an admitted attorney or advocate acting in a professional capacity. A communication from an AI tool is not a communication from a legal adviser.
2. **Litigation privilege argument is weak** — even where AI output is generated for use in litigation, privilege requires a communication between the principal and a third party. AI output is not a communication from a third party in the legal sense.
3. **Copyright uncertainty** — SA copyright law requires a human author (*Copyright Act 98 of 1978 s1*). The legal status of AI-generated work is uncertain, which has knock-on implications for the characterisation of such output as a "document" for discovery purposes.

### 4.2 When Privilege May Attach to AI-Assisted Work

Privilege is likely to attach to AI-assisted work product **only once** a legal practitioner has:

1. Reviewed the AI-generated output in full.
2. Applied their professional skill and judgement to verify the accuracy, relevance, and soundness of the output.
3. Adopted the output as their own professional work product (which may involve editing, supplementing, or replacing portions of the AI output).

The supervision review step is therefore not merely a quality gate — it is the step that transforms unprotected AI output into potentially privileged legal work product.

### 4.3 SA Courts and AI-Generated Fictitious Citations

South African courts have demonstrated zero tolerance for AI-generated fictitious citations:

| Case | Holding |
|---|---|
| *Mavundla v MEC for Transport, KwaZulu-Natal* | AI-hallucinated cases cited in heads of argument; court imposed punitive costs order on counsel |
| *Northbound Processing (Pty) Ltd v South African Diamond Regulator* | Fictitious AI-generated authority cited; matter referred to Law Society for investigation |

Clinic implication: every AI-generated document used in correspondence or litigation must be reviewed by the supervising attorney specifically for citation accuracy. The candidate must verify every case and statutory reference independently before the document is approved for dispatch.

### 4.4 POPIA and AI Tool Use in the Clinic

Before using any AI tool to process client information, the clinic must assess:

1. **Purpose limitation** — is the use consistent with the purpose for which the client's information was collected?
2. **Data minimisation** — does the AI tool require more personal information than is necessary?
3. **Third-party processing** — is the AI tool provider a third-party operator under POPIA? If so, an operator agreement (POPIA s21) is required.
4. **Transfer outside RSA** — if the AI tool processes data on servers outside South Africa, an adequacy or consent basis is required (POPIA s72).

## 5. POPIA Client Data Obligations

### 5.1 The Clinic as Responsible Party

A South African law clinic is a "responsible party" under the Protection of Personal Information Act 4 of 2013 (POPIA) — it determines the purpose and means of processing clients' personal information. The following conditions of lawful processing apply (POPIA s4):

| Condition | Requirement | Clinic application |
|---|---|---|
| Accountability | Responsible party is accountable for compliance | Clinic director or principal attorney is the information officer |
| Processing limitation | Collect only what is lawfully required for the purpose | Intake forms must be reviewed to remove unnecessary fields |
| Purpose specification | Collect for specific, defined, and lawful purposes | Legal services purpose; pro bono referral purposes |
| Further processing limitation | Further processing must be compatible with original purpose | Cannot repurpose client data for research without consent |
| Information quality | Keep information accurate and up to date | File review obligation; update records when circumstances change |
| Openness | Notify data subjects of processing | Client intake disclosure notice required |
| Security safeguards | Protect against loss, damage, destruction, or unlawful access | Secure file storage (physical and electronic); access controls |
| Data subject participation | Respond to access and correction requests | POPIA s23 access rights; s24 correction rights |

### 5.2 Special Personal Information

Clients seeking legal assistance frequently disclose special personal information under POPIA s26–s27:

| Category | Examples in clinic context | Basis for processing |
|---|---|---|
| Health or medical data | Disability accommodation requests; personal injury matters; HIV status in employment matters | Explicit consent (POPIA s26(1)(a)) or legal obligation (s26(1)(b)) |
| Criminal history | Background disclosed in support of expungement applications; prior convictions relevant to sentencing advice | Legal obligation or explicit consent |
| Biometric information | Identity documents with biometric data | Processing limited to identity verification only |
| Trade union membership | Labour matters involving shop stewards | Relevant to the legal matter; explicit consent required |

The clinic must have explicit consent from the client or a clear legal obligation basis before processing special personal information. A general intake consent form is insufficient for special categories.

### 5.3 Retention and Destruction

The clinic must not retain personal information beyond the period necessary for the legal matter and any applicable prescription period:

1. **Active matter files** — retained for the duration of the matter.
2. **Closed files** — retained for a minimum of 5 years (aligning with Prescription Act prescription periods for most civil claims), or longer where the matter involves minors (until three years after majority) or ongoing orders.
3. **Destruction** — files must be securely destroyed (shredding for physical files; secure deletion for digital files) after the retention period. The clinic must document the destruction.
4. **Client access** — clients have the right to access their personal information held by the clinic (POPIA s23). The clinic must have a procedure for responding to access requests within 30 days.

## 6. Supervision-Related High-Risk Flags

| # | Flag | Level | Check |
|---|---|---|---|
| 6 | Supervision ratio breach | 🟠 | Current candidate count per supervisor. Flag at 5, hard block at 7. LPC Rule 37 proviso caps at 6 candidates per admitted attorney supervisor. |
| 12 | Unauthorised practice by candidate | 🟡 | Has any client-facing output been dispatched without passing through the supervision gate? Is any candidate signing correspondence without supervisor countersignature or approval? LPA s34(8)(b)(i) requires service to be rendered under supervision. |
| 13 | Supervisor unavailable without substitute | 🟠 | Is the designated supervising attorney absent without a replacement admitted attorney taking active supervisory responsibility? LPC Rule 37.1.1 requires direct personal supervision — absence without substitute breaches this requirement. |
| 14 | AI output used without privilege review | 🟡 | Has any AI-generated content been included in client advice, court documents, or correspondence without the supervising attorney reviewing, verifying, and adopting it? AI output is not privileged and may contain fictitious citations (*Mavundla v MEC; Northbound Processing v SA Diamond Regulator*). |
| 15 | Special personal information without consent basis | 🟠 | Is the clinic processing health data, criminal history, or other special categories without explicit consent or a clear legal obligation basis? POPIA s26–s27 requires a specific basis for special personal information. |
| 16 | PVTC not LPC-registered | 🟠 | Is the candidate's PVTC registered with the LPC? An unregistered PVTC means the training period does not count toward admission requirements and the candidate is not covered by the supervision framework. |
