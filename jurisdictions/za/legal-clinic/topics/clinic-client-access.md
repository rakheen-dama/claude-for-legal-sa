# Client Access and Referral Framework — South African Law Clinics

This overlay covers the client access, eligibility, language, and referral frameworks applicable to South African university law clinics. It is loaded by client-intake, cold-start-interview, and ramp skills when jurisdiction = ZA.

---

## Legal Aid South Africa — Referral Interface

Legal Aid South Africa Act 39 of 2014 establishes Legal Aid SA as the national public entity responsible for providing legal aid to persons unable to afford legal representation. The objects of the Act include providing legal aid, ensuring state-funded representation in proceedings where failure to provide legal aid would result in substantial injustice, and promoting legal awareness.

**Means test — eligibility:**

- Eligibility is determined by a two-part test: household income (gross monthly income of the applicant and contributing household members) and assets (excluding a primary residence below a threshold)
- The Director-General publishes updated thresholds periodically; practitioners should confirm current figures with the nearest Legal Aid SA justice centre

**Criminal legal aid (Legal Aid SA Regulations 4–8):**

- The primary criterion is whether refusal would result in **substantial injustice**
- High Court and Regional Court accused may qualify without further inquiry into means where the offence is sufficiently serious
- District Court accused qualify for legal aid only in respect of scheduled offences or where substantial injustice would otherwise result
- The relevant public defender is assigned from the nearest justice centre; clinic refers and does not continue as representative once Legal Aid SA accepts the matter

**Civil legal aid (Regulation 9):**

- Three conditions must all be satisfied:
  1. Good prospects of success on the merits
  2. The outcome would be enforceable and beneficial to the applicant
  3. Legal Aid SA has the resources to take on the matter
- A written merit report (prepared by the referring clinic or practitioner) is required; the report must address all three conditions
- Legal Aid SA has discretion to decline even where conditions are met if resource constraints apply

**Special categories:**

- Equality court cases: legal aid may be granted (Regulation 21) regardless of means, where the matter raises a significant equality right
- Children: a child may qualify for legal aid independently of the parent's or guardian's means in certain circumstances (Regulation 24), particularly in care and protection proceedings and cases involving the child's own rights

**Justice centres:**

- Legal Aid SA operates a national network of justice centres and satellite offices in all provinces; most large Magistrate's Courts have a Legal Aid SA office in the court building
- Referral process: clinic assesses the client's matter and confirms it is beyond clinic capacity or resources; clinic issues a referral letter setting out the matter, steps taken, and the reason for referral; client takes the letter to the nearest justice centre

## Free Services Requirement

Law clinics operate under a statutory prohibition on charging for services rendered to clinic clients.

- Legal Practice Act 28 of 2014 (LPA) s34(8)(c): legal services rendered by a law clinic or community-based paralegal advice office **must be free of charge** to the recipient
- **Exception:** a clinic may recover amounts actually disbursed on behalf of the recipient, including court filing fees, sheriff's service fees, and the costs of obtaining certified copies of documents; these must be actual out-of-pocket costs and not a mark-up
- LPA s34(8)(b)(ii): no fee sharing, commission, or partnership-like profit arrangements are permitted in connection with clinic services; a supervisor who is also in private practice may not receive a referral fee or any financial benefit from clinic matters
- Contravention of the fee prohibition exposes the clinic director and the supervising legal practitioner to LPC disciplinary proceedings

## Clinic Eligibility Criteria

Unlike Legal Aid SA, law clinics do not operate under a formal statutory means test. Eligibility is determined by each clinic's own policy, subject to the overarching statutory requirement that the clinic serve persons who cannot afford private legal representation.

**Practical criteria commonly applied:**

- Client cannot afford private legal representation (self-declaration is typically accepted; clinics do not conduct formal financial investigations)
- Matter falls within the clinic's subject matter mandate (some clinics are general; others are specialist — family law, refugee law, housing)
- No conflict of interest with an existing or former clinic client
- Matter has reasonable prospects of a legally meaningful outcome (purely advice-only matters are usually accepted regardless of complexity)
- Geographic focus: most clinics serve the community in proximity to the university; clients travelling long distances may be referred to a closer clinic

**Common walk-in profiles:**

- Low-income individuals and families from surrounding townships and informal settlements
- Domestic workers (labour disputes, employment contracts)
- Unemployed individuals facing eviction, debt enforcement, or maintenance defaults
- Refugees and asylum seekers requiring documentation or administrative law assistance
- University students and staff (some clinics extend services to the campus community)
- Elderly persons without family support (pension grants, maintenance, care disputes)

## Language Access

South Africa has **11 official languages**: English, Afrikaans, isiZulu, isiXhosa, Sesotho, Setswana, Sepedi (Northern Sotho), Tshivenda, Xitsonga, isiNdebele, and siSwati.

**Constitutional and statutory foundation:**

- Constitution of the Republic of South Africa, 1996 s6(3): government and organs of state must use official languages for government purposes, taking into account usage, practicability, expense, regional circumstances, and the balance of the language needs and preferences of the population
- Constitution s35(3)(k): every accused has the right to be tried in a language that the accused understands or, if that is not reasonably possible, to have the proceedings interpreted; this right extends to preparatory proceedings and advice

**Practical obligations on clinics:**

- Capture the client's preferred language at the initial intake interview; record this in the case file
- Determine which languages the assigned candidate attorney and supervising practitioner can communicate in; if there is a gap, arrange for interpretation before the first substantive consultation
- Key documents delivered to the client — advice letters, affidavits, court notices, summonses — should be explained in the client's preferred language; where clinic resources permit, translate plain-language summaries
- Client consent forms (POPIA s11) and intake documentation should be available in the dominant languages of the communities the clinic serves

**Interpreter access:**

- For court proceedings: the court is obliged to provide a qualified interpreter; confirm that an interpreter is available before the hearing date and that the interpreter covers the client's specific language and dialect
- For consultations: where clinic staff cannot cover a language, approved community interpreters may be used; ensure interpreter confidentiality agreements are in place before the consultation
- Machine translation tools may assist with document preparation but must not be used as the sole basis for advice or translation of client-facing documents without practitioner review

## Referral Pathways

| Client need | Referral to | Contact method |
|---|---|---|
| Criminal representation beyond clinic capacity or resources | Legal Aid SA — nearest justice centre | Walk-in with referral letter, or clinic-to-clinic referral call |
| Labour or employment dispute (unfair dismissal, unfair labour practice) | CCMA — Commission for Conciliation, Mediation and Arbitration | File referral at nearest CCMA office; CCMA referral form available online |
| Unfair discrimination (race, gender, disability, etc.) | Equality Court (located at every Magistrate's Court) | Application to clerk of court; no filing fee |
| Human rights violation | South African Human Rights Commission (SAHRC) | Complaint via SAHRC regional offices or online portal; no fee |
| Small claim under R20,000 | Small Claims Court (at every Magistrate's Court) | Application to clerk of court; no legal representation permitted — clinic advises and assists only |
| Residential rental dispute | Rental Housing Tribunal (provincial) | Complaint form to provincial tribunal; free and no legal representation required |
| Consumer goods or service complaint | National Consumer Commission (NCC) or Consumer Goods and Services Ombud (CGSO) | Online or telephonic complaint to NCC; CGSO for goods and services sector members |
| Over-indebtedness or debt review | Registered debt counsellor under NCA s86 | Referral to NCR-registered counsellor; client must apply directly |
| Refugee documentation or status | Refugee Reception Office (DHA) or specialist refugee law clinic | Walk-in at nearest RRO; refer to Wits Law Clinic Refugee Unit or UNHCR office for complex matters |
| Social grant dispute or denial | South African Social Security Agency (SASSA) and SASSA Internal Appeals Process | Written appeal to SASSA within 90 days of notification; Independent Tribunal for Social Assistance Appeals if SASSA appeal fails |
| Pension fund dispute | Pension Funds Adjudicator | Written complaint; free; no legal representation required for adjudication |

## Community Service Structures (LPA s29(2))

Work performed at a law clinic counts toward the community service obligations imposed on legal practitioners and candidate attorneys under the Legal Practice Act.

**Structures through which community service may be completed (LPA s29(2)):**

1. Service in a State department, public entity, or institution (approved by the Minister)
2. Service at the SAHRC or a similar constitutional institution
3. Service as a judicial officer (e.g., commissioner in the Small Claims Court)
4. Legal education or training provided for and on behalf of the LPC or an accredited academic institution
5. Service at Legal Aid SA
6. Service at an accredited law clinic or community-based paralegal advice office approved by the LPC

**Key points:**

- Full-time law clinic staff (both legal practitioners and candidate attorneys employed full-time at the clinic) are exempt from the separate community service obligation because their full-time clinic work already constitutes qualifying service: LPC Guidelines Regulation 8.2.1
- A qualified legal practitioner who supervises clinic candidates may count the supervision hours as part of their own community service obligation, provided the supervision is substantive and documented
- Candidate attorneys on their articles or vocational training who rotate through a clinic placement accrue community service hours for each day of clinic placement, provided the clinic is LPC-accredited
- The clinic director must maintain records of service hours for each person completing community service at the clinic; these records are submitted to the LPC on request
